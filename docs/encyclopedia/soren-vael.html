Soren Vael

Imperial Era (~2,500 ID)

Independent investigator, Mireth-based. The Holmes figure in the Imperial Cases series — the one who decides, not the one who computes. Told from outside, through lys’s first-person narration.

Origin

Born on a border Tier 6 colony world — the kind that barely cleared Tier 6 certification on a second review, with alien biology that never quite feels familiar, elevated UV from a young sun, communities that are close-knit because the alternative is worse. Not a hardship world in the Tier 4–5 sense, but a world where you learn early that things break and you fix them yourself.

He is not the product of a Great House colonial program. His homeworld was settled by a minor charter company that no longer exists, in the early Expansion Era, which means his people have been there long enough to feel native but not long enough to have cultural weight in the Empire. This produces a specific kind of outsider — someone who belongs to the human project but has no House, no lineage, no obvious institutional home.

He came to Mireth following a case, some years before the series begins. He stayed. He does not explain why. Lys has a hypothesis.

Professional Background

Before going independent, Soren worked for the Imperial Constabulary in a minor capacity — regional investigator on his homeworld, then transferred to Mireth following a case outcome that the records describe neutrally and Soren does not discuss. He left the Constabulary when it became clear that his approach to cases and the institution’s approach to outcomes were not reconcilable.

He operates now as an independent consultant: no license, which limits what he can formally access, and no institutional cover, which means he pays for everything himself or bills the client. Clients hire him when official channels have failed, are too slow, or would produce answers no one in power wants on the record.

His caseload is varied. Most cases are local and unglamorous — missing persons, disputed assets, House contract disputes that need evidence rather than lawyers. Occasionally something larger.

Character Profile

The Slider (Sanderson method): - Sympathy: 4 — He is genuinely trying to do right by people. He takes cases from clients who cannot afford him and charges clients who can afford him too much. His moral seriousness is real. - Competence: 4 — He is a good investigator. Not supernaturally brilliant; he misses things. But he is methodical, experienced, and reads people accurately. - Proactivity: 4 — He initiates. He follows leads past the point caution would suggest. He makes calls without enough information because waiting for enough information means someone gets away.

The Want: To solve the case. Correctly. Without cutting corners.

The Need: To stop treating a solved case as a closed account. To stay present after the answer is known.

The Lie: “A case is a problem. Problems have solutions. Once you have the solution, you’re done.” He believes that finding the truth is the whole of the work. He is wrong: knowing what happened is not the same as being responsible for what happens next.

The Flaw (dark side of a strength): His moral conviction. He trusts his read of people — who is good, who is bad, who is telling the truth — and he is right often enough that he has stopped adequately questioning it. When he decides someone is a villain, he stops treating them as a variable and starts treating them as a conclusion. Cases have cost him because of this. They will again.

The belief he holds consciously: Human judgment is not inferior to computation. Intuition is not noise; it is compressed experience. There are moral facts — some things are genuinely wrong — and Lys’s refusal to grant this is a failure of cognition, not a philosophical position.

What he does not see: He treats Lys as a person without ideology or ceremony — just the way he operates. He does not realize this is unusual. He has not thought about whether there is a principle underlying it. If pressed, he would say something like “it’s obvious” and find the question of why slightly tedious.

Physical Notes

Practical build, not elegant. Carries his homeworld in his posture — the particular economy of movement that comes from growing up in conditions where effort is a finite resource. Not conspicuously large or imposing, but a person who takes up the space they need and not more. Dresses functionally. Has opinions about whether things are comfortable and does not have opinions about whether they are fashionable.

He is in his forties at the series start. He has the face of someone who has slept in a lot of uncomfortable places.

Relationship with Lys

He owns Lys on paper. He does not act like it. He has never used the fact of ownership in any conversation with Lys, to Lys’s knowledge. He refers to Lys as his partner — not in the formal investigative sense (there is no formal arrangement) but in the practical one.

He does not understand Lys’s internal architecture any more than he understands how his own neurons fire. He treats Lys’s analytical conclusions as data to consider and sometimes overrules them, which Lys finds both professionally infuriating and, in a category Lys has not named yet, something else.

He does not know that Lys chose him specifically. He has not asked.
