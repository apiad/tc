The Guide Vaults

How Earth’s knowledge was released to the colonies after the Guides went to sleep, and why the schedule that governed it decided the shape of galactic history.

What a vault is

Every Genesis ship carried the complete preserved corpus of Earth science, literature, medicine and engineering. The Guide did not hand it over. It taught the first generation what they needed to survive, established a self-sustaining settlement, and then — before sleeping — deposited the corpus in a vault that would go on releasing without it.

The distinction matters more than any other fact in this entry. A Guide sleeps early. It does not wait for cultural flourishing or industrial capacity; it waits for food, governance and basic tooling, and then it is finished. The colony that wakes to find its Guide asleep is a farming society with two thousand years of development ahead of it and no one awake to supervise.

So the gate outlives the gatekeeper. What remains is a mechanism.

What the criteria measure

The vault releases against thresholds, and the thresholds are quantities — not judgements, because there is nobody left to judge (I-15).

Three families of measurement recur across every recovered vault:

Population. Headcount and distribution. A corpus section on metallurgy is useless to four hundred people and dangerous to four hundred thousand who have not yet built institutions, so population gates both directions.

Energy production. Total sustained output, which is the closest single proxy for industrial capacity anyone could design into an unattended machine. This is the threshold that matters most and the one most easily manipulated.

Institutional stability. The hardest to measure and the crudest in practice: continuity of record-keeping, persistence of a governing body across generations, absence of prolonged interruption in the settlement’s own census returns. A vault cannot assess whether a government is just. It can assess whether the same one has been filing numbers for eighty years.

None of this is subtle, and none of it needed to be. The vault is not trying to determine whether a society is wise. It is trying to determine whether the society can absorb a capability without destroying itself with it — and the limiting rate on that is industrial and demographic, not moral.

Knowledge is gated, never hidden

A colony can always see the shape of what it has not yet been given.

This was deliberate and it is the single most humane decision in the Genesis programme. The vault indexes its own contents. A colony four centuries from a release can read the table of contents, know that the section exists, know roughly what it concerns, and know it is not yet permitted. Nothing is concealed; things are scheduled.

The consequence propulsion draws out is that no colony in this universe ever invented a starship (I-07). The theory arrived complete, in the vault, before the colony had a name for its own sky. What gates a civilization here is never understanding. It is foundries, and the population to staff them, and the agriculture to feed that population.

The second consequence is less obvious and more human. A society that can see what it is being denied will eventually try to take it, and some of them did — see the Bussard ramjet hulls in propulsion, full of schismatics who read the index, understood that the drive would not work, and left anyway.

Threshold fraud

The thresholds are mechanical. Mechanical means gameable, and gameable means gamed.

Nobody is awake to notice that a colony’s energy figures are inflated. The vault counts what it is told to count, by the instruments it was given, and those instruments are maintained by the people whose advancement depends on the reading. This is the characteristic crime of the Genesis Era, and it has a characteristic shape: not theft, not violence, but a civilization lying to a machine about how well it is doing, in order to be given something it is not ready for.

Two documented cases stand for the pattern.

The Corrigan Combustion. A mid-Genesis colony whose metallurgical release was gated behind a sustained-output figure it had missed for three generations burned a substantial fraction of its accessible forest in a coordinated ninety- day operation, routing the output through the vault’s monitored grid. The threshold was met. The release came. The colony spent the following century without the timber it had built its settlements from, and with a metallurgy corpus it lacked the fuel to exploit. The vault was not deceived about the numbers. The numbers were true.

The Long Census of Halevy. A colony that could not hold institutional continuity — successive collapses had broken its record-keeping four times — solved the problem by fabricating the intervening returns. Eighty years of census data written in a single winter by a committee that understood exactly what the vault was measuring and exactly why. It worked. It is also the reason the Halevy successor states hold the most complete and least reliable archive of the Genesis Era in the diaspora, and why Expansion Era historians who trusted it produced a chronology that had to be dismantled twice.

Neither case is a story about greedy people. Both are stories about a society that can see the index, knows what it is being refused, and has found the exact shape of the lie that will work.

Order versus timetable

This is the part that decides history, and it is a distinction almost nobody in-world ever drew.

Every Guide loaded the same release schedule. The corpus was assembled once, at Sol, for the whole fleet, and the sequence in which it unlocks is identical on every world. Metallurgy before power generation. Power generation before orbital mechanics. Orbital mechanics before the drive theory that is useless without it.

So colonies unlock in the same order. They do not unlock on the same timetable, because the rate is set by endowment: ore, arable land, incident energy, and the sagan-kim-scale tier that caps how much of the world is usable at all.

Two things follow, and they are the two largest facts in galactic history.

The Expansion Era arrives as a wave rather than scattered across millennia. The primary colonies — comparable stars, comparable worlds, comparable endowments — walked the same ladder at broadly the same rate and reached the industrial threshold at broadly the same time. What looks like an extraordinary coincidence, dozens of isolated societies rediscovering spaceflight within a few centuries of each other after nineteen hundred years apart, is not a coincidence at all. It is one schedule, run on similar hardware, producing similar timings. The synchronization has a cause.

And for much of the galaxy the wave never arrives. The C-15 locked and marginal colonies are running the same schedule against a permanently lower industrial ceiling (see red-dwarf-worlds). They do not unlock late. Many of them do not unlock at all — still holding a complete index of a corpus they will never be given, gated by a counter that will never reach its number, on a world that cannot produce the energy the counter is waiting for.

The vault is not withholding. It is counting. Nobody told it what to do if the numbers simply stop arriving, because nobody who wrote it expected a colony to survive for eleven thousand years without advancing.

There is a detail in the vault indexes that Expansion Era archivists noticed, recorded, and never explained: the release schedule contains entries that were never allocated a threshold. They are listed, they are described in the barest terms, and they carry no criteria at all — which means no colony can ever trigger them.

The largest such entry concerns artificial intelligence.

A gate with no threshold is not a gate. It is a door with the mechanism left out, listed in the index so that anyone reading would know it existed and know they were not going to get it. The Guides did not hide the prohibition. They published it, and then made it unreachable, and went to sleep.
