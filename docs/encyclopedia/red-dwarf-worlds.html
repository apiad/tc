Red Dwarf Worlds

Where most of humanity actually lives, and why almost none of its history was written there.

C-04 of the stars within reach of the Exodus are red dwarfs, and the fleet aimed C-07 hulls at the best of them (see cosmography). The colonies that survived became C-15 of the founding settlements — the demographic bulk of the species, on worlds that the Confederacy would later describe, without noticing what it was saying, as the edge of things.

The terminator

A red dwarf is dim, so its habitable zone lies close in. Close enough that tidal forces win.

A world in that zone is locked: the same hemisphere faces the star forever. One side is in permanent day, standing under a sun that never moves. The other is in permanent night, and cold enough on the worst of these worlds that the atmosphere itself weathers out onto the rock. Between them runs the terminator — a ring of perpetual dusk, a few hundred kilometres wide, where the temperature gradient is survivable and the light comes in sideways forever.

Everything lives in the ring.

This is the first fact of a red dwarf world and it shapes every other. A locked world has no temperate band and no polar waste; it has an inside and an outside and a habitable circle drawn between them. Cities are strung along the ring like beads. Agriculture runs in the band where the light is strongest without being lethal, which is the same band everyone wants to live in. Territory on such a world is not an area. It is an arc, and an arc has exactly two directions to expand in, and someone is already there.

The characteristic political geography of the diaspora’s poorer half is a chain of settlements that cannot grow sideways.

A sky with no year in it

There are no seasons. A locked world has no axial tilt worth the name and no meaningful orbital variation, so the light does not change. The sun does not rise, does not set, and does not move. It hangs at a fixed angle that depends only on where you stand, and you can navigate by it the way a sailor navigates by a lighthouse.

Which means there is no year visible in the sky at all.

This is stranger than it sounds and its consequences run deep. A calendar has to be invented rather than observed. On Earth and on the free-rotating worlds, the year is a thing you notice; a child learns it before anyone teaches it. On a locked world the orbital period is an astronomical abstraction that a pre-industrial population has no way to perceive and no reason to care about.

So locked colonies keep time by whatever else recurs. Tidal cycles where there is an ocean and a moon. Crop maturation, which fixes the calendar to the agriculture rather than the other way round. Physiological rhythm, in populations that kept a sleep cycle they no longer had a sun to hang it on. Several kept the ship’s clock — the arbitrary twenty-four-hour period their Guide ran the first settlement on, preserved for millennia as a religious inheritance nobody could account for.

The result is that the diaspora’s poorer half does not share a unit of time with its richer half, and did not until the Expansion Era imposed one. See stellar-calendar for what that imposition cost.

Flares

Red dwarfs are unstable in a way the Sun is not. They flare — irregularly, violently, and across a spectrum that strips atmosphere and sterilises surfaces. A world close enough to be warm is close enough to be scoured.

Nothing about this is survivable by prediction. Flare intervals are not periodic and the warning is minutes at best. What is survivable is architecture: you live under rock, or under water, or under enough engineered mass that the event is a noise overhead rather than a death.

Every long-lived culture on a locked world is a culture that goes underground on short notice and has organised itself around being able to. The shelter is the civic building. The drill is the civic ritual. Populations that took millennia to develop writing had flare protocol before they had law, because a settlement that did not have it did not become a settlement.

And it is not superstition dressed as caution. A society whose central shared experience is the sky occasionally tries to kill everyone, without warning, and the only response is to be already inside produces a theology, and the theology is remarkably consistent across worlds that never met. The star is not benevolent and not malicious. It is awake, and it is indifferent, and you do not stand under it longer than you need to.

Confederacy observers in the Expansion Era recorded these as sun-fearing religions and filed them as primitive. They were engineering documents with liturgy attached.

Energy poverty, which decides everything

The rest of this entry describes texture. This part decides history.

Red dwarfs are dim. A world in the habitable zone of one receives enough energy to be warm and nothing like enough to be rich. Every energy budget on such a world is tighter than its equivalent on a free-rotating world around a G-type sun: less incident light for agriculture, less for any solar infrastructure, a thinner margin for everything built on top.

Less available energy means less foundry capacity. Less foundry capacity means a lower industrial ceiling. And the Guide vaults release against measured industrial thresholds — population, energy production, institutional stability (see guide-vaults).

So a locked colony does not merely develop more slowly. It develops toward a ceiling it may never reach, gated by a mechanism that cannot see why it is failing. The vault is not cruel and not broken. It is counting, and the numbers do not arrive.

This is the mechanism behind the single most consequential asymmetry in galactic history. The mobile colonies unlocked their corpus together, found each other, and built the Confederacy. The locked colonies mostly did not, and were still pre-industrial when the Confederacy arrived — which is why first contact between branches of the diaspora was not a reunion but a discovery, asymmetric from the first day, conducted by people who had a word for what they were doing and had used it before.

See galactic-timeline for what the Expansion Era did with that advantage.

Adaptation

A marginal-flight colony began with a narrower founder population than a primary one — the manifest traded embryo mass for the adaptation corpus and the hardened descent vehicles that would let anyone survive at all (see cosmography).

Narrow founder populations, higher selective pressure, and a world demanding irreversible modification: the outcome is divergence, fast and far. Adapted populations change in stature, in colouring, in build, in the tolerances of the skin and the eye — and because the direction of the change follows the world, no two of them drift the same way. A population shaped by a dim sun and a permanent sideways light does not resemble one shaped by a thin atmosphere and a high-radiation ring, and neither resembles baseline human stock.

Under sagan-kim-scale this is what I-14’s second axis measures. A Tier 5 free world and a Tier 5 locked world are the same tier and not the same place, and they do not produce the same people.

The social consequence is sagan-kim-scale’s to state. The historical one belongs here: these populations cannot leave. Not by policy — biologically. Adapted past compatibility with the worlds their cousins live on, they are bound to the planet that made them, permanently, in a galaxy whose wealth is measured in mobility.

The long inheritance

One thing belongs to these worlds and to nothing else in the diaspora.

A G-type star has a working life of ten billion years. A red dwarf burns for a trillion, and burns steadily for almost all of it. Every mobile colony in the Confederacy is living on borrowed cosmic time by comparison; the locked worlds are not.

The Exodus planners knew this, and it is why C-07 hulls went to targets nobody expected to work. A programme optimising for the next ten thousand years would have sent everything to the good stars. This one did not.

Worth holding while reading the later eras: after the Great Scattering, when the Nexus is gone and the mobile worlds are stranded in fragments, the locked populations are exactly where they always were — on worlds no empire can reach, around stars that will still be burning when every G-type sun in the former Confederacy has gone out.

The Scattering did not scatter them. It left them alone, which is a different thing, and in the very long run it may not be the worse one.
